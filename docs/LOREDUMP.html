Element System

There are six elements, each with a primary battle role:

  • Ahi (Fire) - Fighter: Deal physical damage using martial arts.
  • Lau (Nature) - Healer: Restore health and cure negative status effects.
  • Wai (Water) - Defender: Raise defense and take damage in place of allies.
  • Ra (Light) - Enhancer: Improve allies’ abilities and strength (buffs)
  • Aku (Shadow) - Saboteur: Cause negative status effects (debuffs)
  • Mana (Spirit) - Blaster: Deal magical damage using spells.

Every playable character or class has a combination of two elemental affinities that defines their playstyle, meaning every character fights with a synergy of two strategies. For example; characters with Ahi or Wai are excellent at both attacking and defending, characters with both Lau and Aku both heal and inflict ailments, etc. Some classes can augment abilities of one type with the element of another. For example, while Lau is defined as a physical element normally used for healing, a class that combines Mana with Lau can do Lau elemental magic damage using offensive Mana Spells.

Elements Strengths and Weaknesses

  • Ahi burns Lau
  • Lau drinks Wai
  • Wai quenches Ahi.
  • Ra purifies Aku
  • Aku corrupts Mana
  • Mana transcends Ra.

Connection to the story and characters

The elements also have thematic flavor. Characters with Ahi or Wai affinities are typically martial artists, the latter being more patient and less aggressive in temperament than the former. Characters with Lau are healers, medicinal or magical, and tend to gravitate toward Anuic beliefs or a connection to nature. Characters with Ra are normally either Bandari in ancestry or followers of The Teachings of Shaka. Characters with Aku affinities have a morally grey world view, and characters with Mana are magic users with a bias or devotion toward one member of any pantheon of gods. For example, a Māra priestess has a dual elemental affinity of Ra and Lau, reflecting a syncretic faith reconciling the native animist beliefs of east Nuitai with Shakan moral philosophy. However none of these descriptions are strict rules, they are just symbolism or guiding themes, and the element system itself is not woven into the lore nor is it ever mentioned in the story by the characters themselves.

Article-Sailing-b8e

The player begins in the Māra Iti Islands, and can sail in either a clockwise or counterclockwise direction. Counterclockwise takes longer than clockwise travel (penalty for backtracking), and every trip requires provisions and faces danger. As players advance through the story they will get various ship upgrades. Two upgrades that make ocean travel safer and faster, and a final airship upgrade that can avoid the maelstrom entirely, and provide access to floating islands.

The Great Maelstrom at the center of the map spins faster at the core where it is more dangerous but ships can pick up more speed. The further out from the core of the maelstrom you go, the less intense the current and weather get, and the benefits and penalties of sailing in ether direction are less exaggerated.

Nuitai is about 10,000 miles wide (further out there is no wind and giant predatory sea monsters that make seas too far out from the maelstrom impossible to explore) so any point in Nuitai is about 5000 miles from the core of the maelstrom.

The effects of the maelstrom are gradient however it is normally divided into six zones.

Zones of the Great Maelstrom

  1. Outer Sea (“The Calm Ring”): 3,500 – 5,000 miles from the core. Gentle tides and predictable winds. Navigation easy. Only faint hints of the Maelstrom’s pull. Ideal for trade and island hopping. Clockwise travel is roughly 1.10x the speed, counterclockwise travel is .090x the speed. Effects are barely noticeable for short distances, but cumulative for long voyages.
  2. The Driftbelt (“Mariner’s Drift”): 2,500 – 3,500 miles from the core. Constant, steady clockwise drift. Occasional rough weather but safe for most ships (not counting the freak unpredictable storms that are common in Nuitai, independent of the maelstrom). Clockwise travel is roughly 1.25x the speed, counterclockwise travel is 0.75x the speed.
  3. The Surge Zone (“Sailor’s Run”): 1,500 – 2,500 miles from the core. Strong currents give daring captains incredible speed. The waves grow taller, and navigation requires experience. A favorite test for competitive sailors. Clockwise travel is roughly 1.5x the speed, counterclockwise travel is 0.5x the speed.
  4. The Tempest Ring (“Storm’s Edge”): 1,000 – 1,500 miles from the core. Furious winds, towering waves, and constant lightning. Only legendary ships or those blessed by Moanaku survive long here. Speed doubles, but one wrong turn means disaster. Clockwise travel is roughly twice the speed, counterclockwise travel is ×0.25 the speed.
  5. The Eye’s Reach (“The Abyssal Spiral”): 500 – 1,000 miles from the core. Gravity and magic twist the sea itself. Time and space distort. Ships caught here are either lost forever or reappear months later in strange places. The only sailors that venture here are those with a death wish.
  6. The Core (“The Heart of Moanaku”): 0 – 500 miles from the center. No ship has ever returned. This is certain death.

Document-Shakas Animal Lives-659

"Shaka’s Animal Lives" is a beloved Bandari children’s book said to have been compiled centuries ago by the monk Tamba Uli, who sought to teach the youngest initiates about the Spiral through allegory. Each story recounts one of Shaka’s earlier incarnations, sometimes as a fox or hare, sometimes as a tree learning patience or a human rediscovering kindness. Some of the animals featured in these fables are unknown outside Bandar and have since passed into legend for Matahari-born Bandari, who will never see them in their lives.

One fable has long divided readers. In early versions, Shaka appears as a clever monkey who teaches the gods humility. The tale remains controversial, as some believe Shaka himself was a Vanara Bandari, and its telling often rekindles old debates best left resting.

Ethnicity-Bandari-4d6

The Bandari are a seafaring people hailing from the distant and now-lost country of Bandar, somewhere far to the west of Nuitai. Two centuries ago, their fleets of broad-hulled ships bearing the sun-mark of Shaka crossed the endless ocean, bringing luxury goods, sophisticated craftsmanship, clothing, cuisine, culture, and organized religion that forever altered the island peoples circling The Great Maelstrom .

They settled chiefly in the western seas of Nuitai, on the Matahari Archipelago, founding the radiant city of Kota Emas, their golden beacon of faith and trade. Many also settled in the shadows of the Bayang Seas, south of Matahari, where the lawful Zealots of Bandar have less influence.

They are known for their artistry, cosmopolitan culture, spiritual devotion to The Teachings of Shaka, and seafaring skill. Bandari architecture and clothing favor gold, white, and deep reds; their cities are full of canals, prayer banners, and the scent of burning resin.

The spoken language of the Bandari, Bandhasa, is written using a script characterized by curvy flowing letters punctuated by loops. Nuitai’lo, the language of the native Nuitagata, had no written script before the arrival of the Bandari, relying on oral tradition, and so they the native people adopted this imported script for themselves.

Appearance and Varieties

There are two primary lineages among the Bandari: Manusia and Vanara. Though they share ancestry and culture, the two diverged in form and temperament over centuries.

Manusia

The Manusia are the more common lineage among the Bandari, appearing broadly human yet bearing the indelible mark of their mythic ancestry. They are not too different in appearance from the native Nuitagata, though they lack the imposing height and goliath build of the latter.

Their features are vigorous and striking. Their skin tends toward lighter shades which, unlike that of the Nuitagata, is not traditionally adorned with tattoos. Eye colors range from deep bronze to molten gold. Their hair, in shades of brown, gold, or black, grows full and wild like dancing flames, often dark at the roots and sun-lightened at the tips.

Bushy eyebrows and heavy sideburns frame their faces. Their brows, thick and expressive, often arch or curl in gentle waves reminiscent of stylized sunrays or the rolling motion of fire, lending their faces a vivid, almost luminous intensity.

Despite this, the Manusia take great care to keep their hair neatly trimmed or bound for the sake of appearances. Their overall look is one of restrained vitality, reflecting the dignity and discipline of a people renowned for their learning, order, and faith.

Vanara

Sometimes known as the "Children of the Forest," the rarer Vanara Bandari share much of the same vivid appearance as their Manusia kin. They have the same bold stylish brows, thick sideburns and wild flame like hair with sun-warmed hues that hint at their radiant ancestry. Yet where the Manusia are defined by discipline and poise, the Vanara embody motion and grace.

Smaller and lighter than their Manusia cousins but just as devout and intelligent, Vanara are agile and quick, capable of feats of balance and speed unmatched by other peoples, only the amphibian Ranagata of the Lanuroan rainforests rival their skill for acrobatics. Dense gold and brown fur covers their entire body. They have long prehensile tails, strong enough to hang from tree branches or the rafters and balconies of crowded cities. Their athleticism makes them more naturally talented at channeling Kolindala. Vanara are celebrated as skilled martial artists, spies, and scouts. They are common among the ranks of the Penyamun.

Although Vanara sometimes face discrimination from the Manusia, stereotyped as thieves, both groups generally coexist peacefully. Many believe that Vanara Bandari were more common in the past and have sacred ancestry. Some depictions of Shaka himself show him as small, humanoid figure with a furry tail, suggesting he may have been a Vanara himself. This same art tends to depict Shaka more as a warrior of the people or benevolent trickster rather than a serene sage.

Other Bandari Peoples

In addition to the Manusia and Vanara, there are a variety of other races that trace their ancestry from Bandar and identify as Bandari but through cultural identity rather blood lineage. They are also the descendants of people who sailed to Nuitai two centures ago and have lost contact with Bandar. For this reason "Bandari" can either refer to the race or the civilization.

Many are broadly human in appearance, but lacking the distinctive stylish eyebrows and hairstyles of the Manusia. Others are feline, avian, reptilian, aquatic, simian, and even proboscidean.

Most follow the teachings of Shaka, but there are a variety of minority religion and spiritual traditions practices in Bandari territories as well, the most common of which predate Shaka but have the same cosmological foundations.

This variety of culture and religion can be more easily found in the urban areas of the Matahari islands, especially the cosmopolitan Kota Emas where the Shakan faith takes center stage but minority religions are tolerated. This diversity is also common in lawless underworld of the Bayang Seas. Pirate crews are by far the most diverse microcosms of the people of Nuitai, where ancestry is not considered relevant.

Society and Culture

Bandari life revolves around faith, craft, and discipline. The Teachings of Shaka guide moral conduct, shaping everything from architecture to cuisine.

Meals are rich with spice and balance. Rice is a common staple steamed in coconut milk, grilled fish wrapped in banana leaves, and sweet pandan cakes offered at shrines. These popular foods can be found in lively market stalls along the canals of Kota Emas, along with various fruits and vegetables such as breadfruit and star fruit. Durians are banned due to their smell.

The Zealots of Bandar are famous for their martial art, Sila, blending swordsmanship with meditative movement, reflecting both physical mastery and spiritual clarity. In all things, the Bandari prize refinement, learning, and devotion. They brought modern science, philosophy, literature and scholarship to Nuitai. They are a people as polished and enduring as the golden temples of Kota Emas.

Technology and Craftsmanship

The Bandari are master shipwrights, builders, and artisans. Their broad-hulled jong and narrow lancaran ships are famed across the Maelstrom for their speed and beauty, built from hardwood and lacquered in red, black, and gold.

Bandari cities are threaded with canals, bridges, and tiered temples of stone and stucco, adorned with sun motifs and polished brass reliefs. Skilled metalworkers and weavers, they produce fine kris blades, intricate jewelry, and patterned silk bandanas and sarongs that shimmer like water in sunlight.

Ethnicity-Lanigata-fce

Scattered across the islands of Nuitai are the ruins, temples and statues created by the "sky people," the Lanigata. They vanished four thousand years ago and the only traces left of them are their architecture and magical technology which is still active. The Lanigata discovered how to harness Mana to carve glyphs into stone, allowing them to move stone (elevators, doors), preserve memory, or animate constructs.

The Nuitagata avoid the stone megaliths, for they sometimes awaken and attack anyone who ventures too near. The only warning that stone will move is when the strange symbols carved into the stone begin to hum and glow. The symbols alone were mysterious to a people who until foreign contact recorded and preserved knowledge through oral tradition. The sky is filled with floating islands, the largest of which above a colossal eternal maelstrom is The Floating Continent and the silhouettes of mysterious serpentine beings can be seen flying high above the clouds. Occasionally artifacts or stone golems are known to come falling from the sky. These falling golems are a threat if they are not destroyed at the fall. Some of these giants can be seen on the horizon, walking waist deep in the ocean, a humbling site in addition to the occasional sea beast that pops its head out in the distance.

Ethnicity-Nuitagata-97e

The Nuitagata are the most common native peoples of the islands of Nuitai. Broadly human in appearance, they are a large and hardy folk; tall, strong, and broad of build. It is not unusual for some of them to reach heights of eight feet tall.

Their skin ranges through shades of deep bronze, while their dense, coarse hair falls in heavy waves, ranging in hue from dark brown to fiery red. Their eyes are strikingly varied, with pupils in every color of the spectrum; heterochromia is common. The men grow their hair long but keep it tied back, and facial hair is sparse and usually shaved. It is a longstanding tradition among the Nuitagata to mark their skin with tattoos.

They are the descendants of the first inhabitants to settle the archipelago, long before the arrival of the Bandari or the rediscovery of the ruins of the Lanigata. Their ancestors were unparalleled sailors and explorers. Their communities are scattered across nearly every island chain within the Calm Ring and Driftbelt, from the gentle shores of Māra Iti to the mist-shrouded Makua Archipelago and the golden coast of Matahari.

Spiritually, the Nuitagata traditionally follow the ancestral belief known as Anúa, the native faith of Nuitai. Anúa teaches that the world itself and all things within it are divine, and that everything moves because of Mana the spiritual energy that animates existence, and nothing truly stands still. When missionaries from the distant country of Bandari made contact with these islands and introduced the teachings of Shaka, many Nuitagata slowly began integrating these new ideas with their native beliefs, resulting in a variety of syncretic religions practices around the islands of the maelstrom.

The Nuitagata speak Nuitai’lo, a continuum of closely related dialects descended from a common ancestral tongue. Each island group has its own accent, rhythm, and vocabulary shaped by local tradition, but communication between regions remains possible. Travelers may stumble over idioms or ceremonial phrasing, yet the shared structure of Nuitai’lo allows mutual understanding across the ocean.

Before the arrival of the Bandari, the Nuitagata had no form of writing and relied purely on oral traditions. The glyphs of the ancient Lanigata were a mystery to them. Even today most native Nuitagata still cannot read, the literate among them typically being priests of Shaka or travelers, although this is slowly changing. They have adopted the foreign Bandari writing system, which was not designed for Nuitai'lo, which is resulted in complexity and phonetic inconsistency.

Language-Bandhasa-ff9

The language of the Bandari people. There are regional variations between the north and south of the Matahari Archipelago, as well as distinct dialects spoken in Kota Emas and the Bayang Seas.

Bandhasa has a smooth, syllable-timed rhythm that is evenly paced, and can be described as almost musical in nature. The vowels are as clear and consistent as golden bells ringing in time, and the consonants are generally soft and not heavily aspirated.

The overall sound of Bandhasa is smooth and gentle on the ear. For many listeners, it comes across as calm, deliberate, and friendly, with a melodic balance that mirrors its simplicity in structure and the clarity Bandari culture strives for.

Language-Nuitai’lo-845

Nuitai’lo is the language of the native Nuitagata people. It features a consonant–vowel syllable structure, limited consonant clusters, frequent glottal stops, and a tendency toward reduplication.

Dialectal variation occurs across regions, with the most noticeable phonetic differences found between the eastern and northeastern areas, such as the Māra Iti Islands and the The Lanuroa Archipelago, and the southern and southeastern regions, including The Makua Archipelago and The Fire Islands .

In Māra Iti and Lanuroa, the word for “sun,” “day,” or “light” is pronounced Ra, while in the south it becomes La. This l–r alternation is a hallmark of regional phonetic variation. In a similar way, southern dialects often replace k sounds with t sounds, a shift not found in the north. Interestingly, however, the sacred word for “gods,” Akua, remains consistent throughout Nuitai, and it is rarely rendered as Atua.

Before the arrival of the Bandari, Nuitai’lo lacked a written form, and all record keeping was oral. The Nuitagata later adapted the Bandari script for their language, though it does not perfectly represent its sounds. Despite this, many Nuitagata remain illiterate, reflecting broader social and historical factors rather than linguistic ones.

Law-Akua-bfe

The Akua are the gods of Nuitai. They are personifications or great spirits that embody aspects of Mana. The three primary Akua Nui that traditionally represent the head of the Nuitai pantheon are Lanu, the goddess of life and nature, Mauga, the goddess of fire and change, and Moanaku, the god of the sea and death.

In the world of Nuitai, there are beings regarded as being halfway between the three great Akua Nui and mortals. They are known as the Akua Iti. Sometimes referred to as “demi-gods,” they each embody an aspect or “piece” of a greater Akua Nui. Hau is the wind, Makei is the transition from life to death, Ra is the sun and it's light.

Akua Iti can take human form and interact with mortals, they have magic powers and can pop in and out of existence. They are not stable, some walking the world for centuries before splitting or merging with other Akua Iti. Some are merged aspects of two or all three of the Akua Nui.

The Kame have their own pantheon of Akua which they refer to as the Kamekami.

Law-Mana-000

Mana is spiritual energy. It is existence in motion. It permeates all of Laniakea, the universe. It is both soul-stuff and the energy that makes things move. It is the force that animates the world and spins the sea. It is the breath of existence. Mana flows from place to place, through life, death, and rebirth. Every stone, wave, breath, or thought is Mana taking form. When something burns, grows, or fades, it is Mana transforming. Nothing is truly still. It is not merely magic, but the essence of vitality, action, and awareness itself. Mana is why anything can exist, act, or change at all.

Mana is never created or destroyed. It moves. It shifts endlessly between forms, from body to body, from life to death and into life again. This ceaseless transformation is the great turning of the Spiral. When a being dies, its Mana loosens and drifts into the vast motion of the world, gathering again in time as new form and new life. The Spiral has no beginning and no end. It is the unbroken movement of being itself.

The Ancient Way of the Island People

Anúa is the oldest spiritual belief of the Nuitagata, the common native peoples of Nuitai. It teaches that the world itself is divine and that all things, living or not, are as spirits that share in the same breath of Mana. It is a non-dualistic, monistic world view that does not differentiate the mundane natural world from the divine. In the old ways, before the arrival of the Bandari missionaries and the spread of the teachings of Shaka, there was no Doctrine of Recurrence or moral judgment. Life and death were the rising and falling of Mana through all things, shifting from one form to another without reward or consequence. The Akua were honored as great spirits within this flow, neither above it nor apart from it.

What the Nuitagata call Mana, the people of the Kame Kingdom call Ki. The concept is nearly identical, reflecting the same monistic view of life and motion, and most scholars consider Ki to be a regional expression of the broader Anuic understanding of Mana.

The Great Spirits of Nuitai

Throughout Nuitai, there are many pantheons of deities that embody aspects of Mana. These living forces are known as the Akua, great spirits that personify the natural world. The Akua are not separate from creation but are aspects of the natural world themselves. Lanu, the Akua of life and nature, is the scent of blooming flowers, the touch of wet leaves, and the gentle rain that nourishes all things. Mauga, the Akua of fire and change, is the eruption of volcanoes and the burning passion of the heart. Moanaku, Akua of the sea and the Underworld, is entropy, both the ocean and death itself, and the secrets beneath the waves.

Followers of Anúa believe that all things are imbued with Mana. The Akua are simply those beings whose spirits are vast enough to embody entire forces of nature. To hear a bird's song is to hear Lanu singing. To light a flame is to awaken Mauga. To feel the ocean’s pull or death's door is to touch Moanaku.

The Lost Art of the Sky People

In ancient history, the Lanigata, also known as the sky people, discovered how to carve cryptic glyphs into stone to bind Mana within it. Through these inscriptions they could give matter the semblance of life, creating stone golems and animated constructs, known as Pohaku. These stone golems still wander the world, relics of a forgotten age.

Many believe that the art of binding Mana in this way trapped souls within stone, preventing them from returning to the turning of the Spiral. Others claim that the spirits within the Pohaku are willing guardians, choosing to remain and protect what once was. The truth has been lost with the Lanigata themselves, who vanished four thousand years ago.

Mana in Motion

Today, the power of Mana is called upon in a different way, not through crafting stone magitech but through the movement of ones own body. Though Mana is the motion within all things, most of it flows unconsciously. To channel Mana personally is not to create or extract it, but to consciously and momentarily align oneself with that current and give it form through intention. The body acts as a vessel through which Mana can resonate more clearly. The effort lies not in summoning energy from beyond, but in attuning oneself with the flow that already moves through all things.

As Mana is the source of animation and motion, to harness Mana is to move with it. Channeling is almost always performed through movement, such as martial forms, ritual dances, or rhythmic gestures. Even simple breathing exercises can align the body with the current of Mana. The more skilled the practitioner, the less physical exertion is required to draw power; mastery is the art of effortless alignment.

For most practitioners, channeling Mana enhances the body rather than reshaping the world. Martial artists use it to sharpen their reflexes, strengthen their bodies, and heighten awareness. For those who have mastered the esoteric art of magic, they can literally project Mana outward in the form of spells, shaping energy into flame, light, spark or shadow.

The most well known martial arts in Nuitai that draw Mana are Wai’Kenpō; the defensive water based martial art developed in the Kame Kingdom, and Lelua; a fiery form of acrobatic developed in The Fire Islands that resembles dance as much as martial arts.

Channeling Mana feels like waking up. The senses sharpen, the heart quickens, and the body hums with flowing power. The world seems brighter and more alive. Mana can be used for healing, transformation, destruction, or the summoning of spirits. Typically, the only cost is fatigue, but if a Mana user draws beyond their limits or experience they can be bedridden for days, weeks or worse, possibly even death. For those who harness the darker energies of Mana, or Mana as manifested as flame, there are even yet more risks.

The Spiral and the Sun

Bandari cosmology introduced the Nuitagata to the Spiral, an ancient Sarpic model of the universe describing the ordered motion of existence. In this worldview, all beings are animated by Atoma, the individual soul, carried through life, death, and rebirth by the ceaseless turning of the Spiral. This cycle was originally seen as endless and inescapable.

The philosopher hero Shaka later reinterpreted this framework. Rather than accepting recurrence as an eternal certainty, he taught that Atoma may ascend beyond the Spiral through moral action and the cultivation of wisdom, reframing Sarpic cosmology into a path toward salvation. What had once been an infinite wheel became, in Shaka’s vision, a rising motion of transcendence expressed through illumination, symbolized by Shaka's sun mark.

When the Teachings of Shaka reached Nuitai, many Nuitagata wove these soteriological and moral scriptures into their monistic understanding of Mana without difficulty. The Spiral was seen as a Bandari expression of the world’s natural motion the Nuitagata already believed in, and they regarded Shaka as the radiant personification of the sunlight, an aspect of Mana, practicing devotion to him in parallel with their own Anuic traditions without altering their native beliefs.

Unlike the Nuitagata's unifying concept of Mana, Bandari thought distinguishes Atoma, individual souls, from Kolindala, the current of magical energy that permeates all things. In this framework, light magic arises from refining Kolindala into its purest and most orderly form, and many Bandari spellcasters attribute their radiance to Shaka’s teachings.

Sarpic religion traditionally has Devas, immense and ancient Atoma whose long passage through the Spiral has granted them mastery over Kolindala. Though less fluid and mutable than the Akua of Anuic belief, they embody a similar scale of cosmic being.

Dark Currents

In some places, particularly across the Makua Archipelago in the south, shamans known as Callers have learned to weave the darker energies of Mana. This dark energy is not evil. We all have darkness within us, but it is dangerous to harness for the inexperienced. The Callers use it to commune with spirits of the dead, who may choose to speak or aid the living, and for casting shadow spells in self-defense. These rituals carry risk, for malevolent entities sometimes impersonate the departed, and possession is always a possibility.

The Callers respect Moanaku, the Akua of the Sea and the Underworld, even if they do not pray to him, seeing death as a natural part of life. Unlike followers of Shaka, they reject the concept of the Spiral and the Doctrine of Recurrence, for if all souls are recycled, who then answers their calls? To the Callers, souls do not dissolve back into undifferentiated Mana but persist as individual beings within Moanaku’s drowned realm.

The callers also serve the living, comforting the grieving, tending the corpses of departed loved ones, performing funerary rites and, with the blessing of Makei, guiding spirits to rest through rituals. To them, the eternal presence of death is a reminder to have gratitude for this short, fleeting life.

The Callers were among the groups in Nuitai who refused the Teachings of Shaka when they spread across the islands, a resistance shared by the fire dancers of the volcanic islands of the southeast. Shaka’s reinterpretation of the Spiral contradicted their conviction that the dead do not return but remain in the underworld.

Ironically, although the Callers preserve one of the oldest surviving denominations of Anua, their view of the soul is far closer to the Bandari concept of Atoma than to the monistic Anuic worldview held by most Nuitagata, aligning them metaphysically with the very outsiders whose influence they rejected.

Regardless of culture or belief, all magic requires movement. Mana only responds to motion. Each tradition expresses this through its own forms, such as martial arts or ritual dancing, but no one can harness the power of Mana while still.

Location-The Great Maelstrom-916

The central landmark of Nuitai is a striking and awe-inspiring feature: a giant, seemingly never-ending whirlpool, the core of which stretching over thousands of miles across the ocean. This is the great maelstrom, often simply called the Maelstrom, and it dominates both the seas and the skies of this region. The core is the point of no return, nut its vast currents ripple outward for thousands of miles beyond the core, shaping tides, weather, and even the very climate of the island chains surrounding it. Gentle enough at its outer edges to allow navigation, the direction of the currents caused by the great maelstrom encourages clockwise travel around the seas of Nuitai. This effect is exaggerated the closer one gets to the center. The waters grow increasingly chaotic as one approaches the center, with spiraling eddies, rogue waves, and hurricane-force winds forming in the intermediate zones. Here, only the most skilled and daring sailors dare to venture, skimming along the outer edges like surfers riding a colossal wave to gain speed. Entering too far inward brings the point of no return, where even the most powerful ship is drawn inexorably toward the maelstrom’s heart. Sailors respect the maelstrom’s might, offering prayers to Moanaku before venturing into its outer currents.

The Maelstrom also carries the ocean’s life in its currents. Its outer edges are rich fishing grounds, teeming with fish and other sea life swept in by upwelling waters, although larger predators patrol these zones, drawn by the abundance. The strange turbulence affects weather far beyond its surface: storms gather above, winds twist unpredictably, and the flow of heat through the seas is disrupted, creating pockets of unusually warm or cold water. Scattered islands ring the maelstrom, their arrangement forming a loose, circular pattern that has long been theorized to result from some ancient calamity tied to the whirlpool’s creation.

Organization-Māra-a49

Māra is one of the most widely practiced spiritual traditions among the Nuitagata peoples of the eastern seas. While the practice of both Anua and Shaka worship in paralell are common, Mara attempts to make the fusion explicit in its doctrine.

Mara originated on the Māra Iti Islands (hence the name) through the teachings of the Priests of Māra, who sought to reconcile their native animist faith, Anúa, and their devotion to Lanu with the doctrines of light and recurrence brought by Bandari missionaries devoted to the Teachings of Shaka. The resulting synthesis regards all natural life as sacred while also embracing the purifying and illuminating aspects of Shaka’s light. One can be reborn into Shaka's light world by devoting oneself to not just his teachings, but also Lanu and her creations in this world. Some interpretations consider Shaka to be an Akua Iti or aspect of Lanu as her light, a re-interpretation of the native sun god Ra Lanu.

Māra spread from Māra Iti across the The Lanuroa Archipelago and is more prominent in the northeast of Nuitai, becoming less common further south.

The practice varies by island but retains the same symbolism. Priests wear robes of green and gold, dance beneath forest canopies, and recite prayers facing both the rising sun and bowing to the forests. Offerings of fruit, woven garlands, and clean water are made to honor Lanu’s gentle breeze and Shaka’s warm light. Though rooted in pacifism, Māra is not passive or esoteric. Its followers teach that harmony must be tended like a garden, through patience, humility, and labor.

A minority of traditionalists in Mara Iti, known as the Maki Sages, reject the inclusion of Shaka into the pantheon of Akua and continue to devote themselves only to Lanu.

Organization-Sarpa-141

Sarpa is the ancient cosmological and religious tradition of the Bandari people, predating The Teachings of Shaka. It formed the spiritual foundation upon which Shaka later built his philosophy.

Sarpa teaches that all existence moves within an endless cycle known as The Spiral, an unbroken chain of life, death, and rebirth. Unlike the monistic Anúic belief of the Nuitagata, which treats all being as manifestations of a single substance called Mana, Sarpa makes a distinction between Atoma and Kolindala.

Atoma is the individual soul, the enduring self that is reborn from life to life, carrying merit, memory, and identity through what the Bandari call eternal recurrence. Kolindala, by contrast, is the radiant current of magical vitality that permeates the cosmos. It does not reincarnate, nor does it possess selfhood. Instead it is the universal energy that mages, martial artists, and mystics can refine or channel. To the Nuitagata, all of this is Mana.

In Sarpic thought, an Atoma moves through the Spiral within the current of Kolindala, just as a traveler moves within a river: one is the voyager, the other the medium through which passage is made.

The serpent is the central symbol of Sarpa, representing the continuity and sacred movement of life. Snakes are considered holy and protected animals, embodying wisdom, renewal, and the Spiral itself. The faith’s emblem is a serpent coiled in a spiral.

Matahari, the sun god later associated with Shaka, was once a prominent Sarpic deity. Followers of Sarpa still venerate Matahari as the living sun and do not equate him directly with Shaka. While most Sarpic adherents respect Shaka as a great teacher, they view him as mortal or, in some sects, as an avatar or emanation of Matahari rather than a divine being in his own right.

Sarpa is not a single organized religion but a family of related traditions that revere different deities, known as Devas, from the old Sarpic pantheon. Each denomination focuses on a different Deva and has a different interpretation of their role in the cosmic order. Though the Teachings of Shaka have become the dominant and central faith in Bandari civilization, Sarpic communities persist as minorities, maintaining the older rites, serpent symbolism, and eternal cosmology that once defined ancient Bandari spirituality.

Although ancient Sarpa once employed a caste-like system of social roles, modern Sarpic communities no longer follow it. Followers of Shaka often claim that the abandonment of caste was one of Shaka’s greatest moral victories, but Sarpic adherents reject this interpretation. They argue that the original system was never meant to enforce hereditary hierarchy, but to guide individuals toward roles suited to their character and talents. In their view, it was only later historical distortions that hardened it into a rigid class structure. Contemporary Sarpic orders formally renounce caste as outdated, holding that the ancient ideal was one of balanced contribution rather than inequality.

In the crowded markets of Kota Emas, it is common to see Sarpic fortune tellers and snake handlers offering readings of one's destiny within the Spiral. Some read patterns in a serpent’s movements, others read palms or cast die, and a few keep small shrines where passersby ask questions while a sacred snake rests coiled nearby. Sages fall into deep meditation amid swirling incense, interpreting the shapes that form in the drifting smoke. Others use rhythmic drumming or looping dance steps, drawing on Kolindala to enter a state of trance before giving counsel.

These practices and small performances are part of everyday life for many Sarpic communities, blending spirituality with street spectacle, and earning a living. Yet they sit uneasily with devout followers of Shaka. To them, relying on omens risks clouding moral judgment, and consulting a diviner is sometimes viewed as a quiet breach of Shakan discipline. Even so, the booths remain busy, and people often seek guidance discreetly despite the lingering controversy.

Organization-The Teachings of Shaka-1c8

The Teachings of Shaka form the foundation of a major faith originating in the country of Bandar, west of Nuitai. The religion centers on the moral and spiritual philosophy attributed to Shaka, a mortal teacher whose illumination one thousand years ago revealed the nature of recurrence, defined moral conduct, and paved a path of liberation from the cycle of physical existence. This religion is a moral reinterpretation, and sometimes considered a branch, of the much older Sarpic tradition.

Core Beliefs

According to Shaka’s teachings, all souls, Atoma, are continuously recycled upon death. Life and death form a single, unbroken spiral of recurrence. While recurrence was a novel concept to the Nuitagata before the arrival of the Bandari, it was already a core cosmological assumption in Bandar for centuries before Shaka merely framed the Spiral as a prison that could be escaped by following the moral path he described. By devoting oneself to the principles taught by Shaka, the believer may escape this cycle and be reborn into Shaka’s realm of light, a paradise beyond material existence.

Temples, ritual observances, and pilgrimages structure daily life. The religion provides moral guidance, communal cohesion, and a framework for understanding both life and the mysteries of death.

In Nuitai, the principal site of pilgrimage is Kota Emas, and it is customary for worshipers to bow in its direction during daily prayer.

Spread and Syncretism

When the Bandari first arrived in Nuitai, they settled on the golden coasts of the Matahari islands and founded the settlement of Kota Emas. From there the teachings slowly spread to the islands surrounding the Great Maelstrom through trade and missionary activity, and were adapted by many of the island cultures of Nuitai.

The peoples of Māra Iti, the Lanuroa Archipelago, and the Matahari Archipelago integrated the new faith with their native Anuic traditions, some incorporating Shaka as an Akua Iti of light and moral order, identifying him with Ra Lanu.

The Kame adopted his doctrines and integrated it with their discipline of Wai'Ki, emphasizing meditation and personal mastery.

In contrast, the Makua Archipelago and the Fire Islands rejected Shaka’s teachings altogether, maintaining adherence to older Anuic and ancestral practices.

This contact produced a wide range of syncretic variations across Nuitai, collectively referred to as the Shakan Paths.

Schools of Thought

As the religion matured in its originating country of Bandar, two principal interpretations developed: one devotional and exoteric, the other philosophical and introspective. Both claim continuity with the original Bandari scriptures but differ in emphasis and method.

The Path of the Golden Sun

The most widespread form of the faith, often practiced by the general population of both Bandar and Nuitai. It emphasizes devotion, ritual purity, and salvation through Shaka’s grace. Followers believe that moral conduct, prayer, and pilgrimage lead the soul to be reborn into Shaka’s realm. Communal worship, purification rites, and festivals of light are central to practice.

This path is the foundation for most syncretic expressions of the religion across Nuitai, where it has blended with local beliefs and Akua worship. Soteriological and comforting in nature, it is no wonder that this version of the teachings spread like wildfire among the poor and downtrodden masses.

For the native Nuitagata of the northeast in particular, Shaka has literally ascended to the status of an Akua and joined as a newcomer to the traditional pantheon, representing the light of the sun, previously the domain of Lanu, the goddess of life and nature. While the Path of the Golden Sun is more common than its counterpart everywhere, it has an even stronger foothold in the eastern half of Nuitai where the native population is largest, particularly in Māra Iti, the Lanuroa Archipelago.

The Path of the Polished Mirror

A less common but influential school focused on personal cultivation and direct understanding. Adherents teach that enlightenment is achieved not through divine intervention but through inner realization of truth. The “mirror” symbolizes the mind cleared of illusion, reflecting the world as it truly is.

The Path of the Polished Mirror appeals primarily to scholars, mystics, and individuals of education or privilege. It is particularly associated with urban centers such as Kota Emas and with the Kame, who have incorporated its principles into their discipline of Wai’Ki.

This school does not deny the existence of Shaka’s realm but interprets it as a metaphor for awakening, the state in which the individual perceives the unity of all things and transcends recurrence through understanding rather than faith. Some even go as far to as claim that Shaka himself was no different than any mortal and did not ascend himself, treating the teachings more as philosophy than faith, although this strictly secular interpretation is a minority view even within the Path of the Polished Mirror itself, and traditionalists of these school accuse the secular minority of giving the Polished Mirror a heretical reputation.

The Path of the Polished Mirror is more common in the cosmopolitan western half of Nuitai, where the settled Bandari population is largest, particularly in Kota Emas. This is the school of Shaka's teachings that the Kame integrated into their practice of Wai'Ki, the shelled inhabitants of the Kame Kingdom found its patient contemplative nature more fitting to their world view and way of life.

Contemporary Practice

Today, both paths coexist across the Maelstrom. The more common Path of the Golden Sun dominates public and rural worship all over Nuitai, while the more esoteric Path of the Polished Mirror persists in monasteries, academic circles, and the higher clergy in cosmopolitan Kota emas.

Doctrinal disputes between the two schools are rare but enduring. In history, their relationship wasn’t purely antagonistic or separate; it was fluid, overlapping, and occasionally tense. Both paths still honor Shaka and follow the same core doctrines, and it is not uncommon for adherents might move between both paths over a lifetime.

Raglaya governs music and song, Ruplaya presides over the visual arts, including painting, sculpture, color, and the shaping of beauty. Kathlaya rules storytelling, recitation, drama, and theater. Raglaya and Kathlaya are sometimes combined to represent poetry. Karlaya embodies craftsmanship and the transformative skill of shaping raw material into purposeful form.

In their unified iconography, Laya rests cross-legged on a cushion, their eight arms each bear a tool of creation, arranged from the most ethereal arts to the most grounded. Their uppermost pair of hands plays a long-necked lute, the two arms working together as if caught in mid-performance, embodying Raglaya’s music. The next pair holds the instruments of Kathlaya: in one hand a scroll, representing the written word, and in the other a stylized theater mask, its expression shifting slightly from depiction to depiction. The third pair is given to Ruplaya, the visual arts, with one hand lifting a slim painter’s brush and the other supporting a shallow palette marked with swirling colors. The lowest pair belongs to Karlaya, where Laya’s hands hold a broad varnish brush in one and a small bowl of lacquer in the other, tools of finishing and transformation that root the deity’s radiance in the everyday work of artisans.

In northern Matahari and in cosmopolitan Kota Emas, these names are often shortened with either the second vowel dropped off, the -ya ending silent, or both, resulting in the truncated Ragla, Rupla, Kathla, and Karla or Karyala. In the southern islands and extending to the Bayang Seas the longer forms Ragalaya, Rupalaya, Kathalaya, and Karyalaya are more commonly used.

Across Sarpic and Bandari communities, Laya is experienced almost as a muse. To play good music is to let Raglaya play through your hands, to tell a riveting story or recite beautiful poetry is to let Kathlaya speak through you. Laya's inspiration is not limited to just the performing arts, any spark or stroke of inspiration or creativity in any form can be thought of as Laya's blessing.

Although devotion to Laya is found everywhere in western Nuitai, it flourishes most in Kota Emas where performers, poets, and craftsmen treat the Deva of the arts as patron and guiding spirit. Shrines devoted specifically to their musical or storytelling aspects are common in theaters, academies, and artisan districts. In smaller villages, Karlaya is the aspect most often honored, especially among woodworkers, potters, and metalworkers. It is also common for rural shrines to merge Karlaya with Kartava, honoring the two together under the name Karya.

Laya’s domain meets Tavaya’s wherever beauty and function intersect. Their relationship is not antagonistic but complementary: Laya inspires and elevates, Tavaya shapes and refines. Traditions in Kota Emas often interpret this as a partnership between impulse and discipline, while others like to think of Tavaya as a more modern successor to Karlaya’s ancient role in guiding artisans.

Pronounced with a rolled "r", Priya is an ancient Deva associated with love in its different forms.   • Kamapri represents romantic excitement, passion, longing and attraction.
  • Kayapri governs partnership, marriage and the steady bonds of shared life.
  • Karunapri expresses kindness, generosity, compassion and care for friends, family and the larger world.

Person-Shaka-2f1

Shaka is the founder and central figure of the foreign Bandari faith known as the Teachings of Shaka. He is the first Luminary, and possibly the most important figure in all of Bandari culture, at least among the diaspora in western Nuitai.

Mortal in origin and not god in the traditional sense, Shaka was born into a culture shaped by the ancient Sarpic faith of Bandar. The Sapric deities, the Devas, embodied moral and philosophical ideals rather than the elemental forces of nature represented by the Akua of Nuitai. Among theves Devas, Matahari was revered as the light of truth and cosmic order. Shaka’s own teachings would later reinterpret and ultimately inherit those same domains.

He died over one thousand years ago. For many he is ascended and is represented by the light of the sun. He is said to have mantled the ancient Sarpic Deva of the sun, Matahari. In popular tradition this mantle blurred into full identification, leading some to regard Shaka as Matahari reborn in mortal form. He is still sometimes referred to by Matahari’s name, though it is an old epithet in this century. In taking on this mantle he inherited the Deva’s ancient domains of law, order, and clarity, while adding themes of compassion, progress, enlightenment, wisdom, and social justice.

Some Bandari, particularly of Vanara ancestry, claim Shaka was a Vanara himself and depict him in art and sculpture as having a furry prehensile tail. The Vanaran depiction emphasizes compassion, justice, and will rather than serenity, law, and order.

In Nuitai, Shaka is sometimes referred to as "the Ragata" which means "Sun Man" in Nuitai’lo. Many Nuitagata equate him with their native sun god Ra Lanu, an interpretation formalized by the syncretic Māra faith, which reconcile Anúic devotion to Lanu with the teachings of Shaka.

Shaka the Explorer

Before he found wisdom and ascended to become the teacher of the world, Shaka was a man of boundless appetite for adventure and knowledge. Bandari culture is filled with tales of his voyages on the ocean, sailing every known sea, chasing storms for sport and diving into the maws of sea serpents just to see what waited inside. He wrestled monsters, stole from tyrants, and laughed in the face of danger. His strength was inhuman, his cunning unmatched, his bravery and mirth were unrivaled. His shipmates admired him as if he were a demigod, following him to the end of the world and would have gladly followed him into the jaws of underworld itself if he asked it of them.

Shaka the Champion

As the years passed by, somewhere between the triumphs and adventures, Shaka began to laugh less. In the markets and ports he passed through on his journeys, he began to see the weight others carried; the sadness, the hunger, the cruelty, the hollow obedience to masters and gods alike. The injustice and acceptance of suffering angered him. When a famine struck one island, he gave his spoils to feed strangers. When he saw soldiers beating a beggar in the streets for sport, it infuriated him and he bloodily intervened. He changed from a daring laughing explorer without a care in the world to a selfless hero with the weight of the world on his shoulders and seething rage in his heart. Stories say he fought for justice as fiercely as he once fought for gold and adventure. In those times he came to be loved by the common people and the downtrodden, and hated by those in power. He became a hero of the common man, but despite his selfless achievements the bitter anger against the unfairness of it all continued to grow in his heart.

Shaka the Wanderer

Victory after victory Shaka could not find peace. For every suffering he eased there was always more. It never-ending. He came to the melancholy realization that there will always be injustice, always people impoverished and struggling, laboring in the fields only to succumb to disease, old age and then death. Happiness is a fleeting dream. One morning, Shaka is said to have set his sword in the sand, shed his armor, and walked inland alone. He traded his silks for ragged robes, sold his ship, and gave everything away. Then he just walked for days, with no direction in mind. He settled on a beach, stared off into the sunset and began meditating, focusing on his breath, trying to connect to his surroundings and the present moment. This was a common Bandari practice at the time, one he learned as a warrior to hone his senses, but this time he had an epiphany.

Shaka the Luminary

Listening to the gentle tide roll in and out, and watching the rippling reflection of sunlight on the water, he began to appreciate rather than bemoan the impermanence of life. He realized that all things rise and fall like waves, and that to hate the storm is to drown in it. His deeds had meaning, his compassion still mattered, yet he understood his work would never be finished, and that was alright. What mattered was to act without clinging to the result.

The key, he found, was compassion joined with clarity: to see the world’s suffering without turning away, but also without letting anger or despair rule the heart. Acceptance was not surrender, it was the ground from which true courage could grow.

In that reflective stillness something changed within him. The world seemed to slow down. He saw beyond the horizon, and in that vision, every life he had lived. His wisdom swelled with the passing of the ages, and he came to understood that the prison of the Spiral* is built by resistance, the harder one fights the current, the longer one is trapped. Only by yielding to its flow could one pass beyond the Spiral of suffering. He smiled at the setting sun, and in that moment he awakened. Years later, when he died, the Bandari say he ascended with the golden sunlight of the breaking dawn.


It is important for the reader to understand that long before Shaka’s time the people of Bandar already believed that one is reborn into a new body upon death, and that actions in this life influence one’s rebirth. This idea is an ancient Sarpic concept, not something introduced by Shaka. What Shaka did was moralize and reinterpret this older belief into what he called the Doctrine of Recurrence. While these ideas were new to the people of Nuitai when Shaka’s teachings first spread around the maelstrom, they had been part of Bandari cosmology for centuries.

What was truly novel in Shaka’s message was the claim that one could escape and ascend beyond the Spiral entirely. This possibility was subversive and deeply attractive to the poor and lower castes, since it offered a loophole around accumulating merit and rising within the caste system. It meant one could leave the cycle altogether rather than participate in it. Because of this, Shaka remained an enemy to those in power and was hunted by assassins until his death, which was anticlimactically caused by choking on a piece of breadfruit in his old age.

Person-Tavaya-d3b

Tava, known in the southern Matahari islands and the Bayang seas as Tavaya, is the Bandari Deva of engineering, invention, and the beauty found in purposeful creation. Where Laya embodies inspiration and artistic impulse, Tava represents the discipline that refines those impulses into tools, structures, and machines that can endure the world. He is revered as the patron of craftsmen, builders, and innovators, especially those whose work bridges function and elegance.

Tava is always depicted as male, broad-shouldered and burly, with arms thick from labor. His hair is wild and unkempt, and his brows are famously heavy, a trait celebrated among Bandari artisans as a sign of focus and determination. Yet his face is rarely shown directly. In most depictions he is hunched over a workbench or leaning over a half-finished hull, too absorbed in the task before him to acknowledge the viewer. This is not secrecy but devotion to the act of creation, a humility that contrasts sharply with the radiance of the older Devas. Unlike the ancient gods, Tava is never shown with the distinct furry tails of the Vanara. Their absence is often interpreted as a sign of his relative youth, a divine figure shaped by a world rapidly advancing in craft and technology.

Tava embodies two primary aspects. Kartava governs beauty in craft, the elegance that emerges when form and function are in harmony. Yantava represents function itself, the mechanics and structure that allow an invention to work. In the southern Bandari territories his longer names of Kartavaya and Yantavaya are more common.

In his iconography Tava bears four muscular hairy arms arranged from refinement to raw labor. The lower pair belongs to Yantava, gripping a carpenter’s mallet and chisel, symbols of force, shaping, and foundational craft. The upper pair reflects Kartava. One hand holds a broad varnish brush and the other a small bowl of lacquer, representing the finishing touches that give an object grace beyond its purpose. Together these tools present a full philosophy of making, from conception to final polish.

Although Tava is honored across Bandari society, his devotion is strongest among Sarpic builders, engineers, shipwrights, and metalworkers. In coastal cities he is the constant companion of sailors and navigators who depend on sturdy hulls, balanced rigging, and clever design to survive the seas. Small offerings of tobacco, sweet fruits, or coiled rope are left at his shrines by those embarking on long voyages, even by some devotees of Shaka. Even some pirates keep a quiet respect for him. They claim that any vessel that surfs the maelstrom without Tava’s blessing eventually learns what happens when care is not taken in owns craft as a shipwright.

Tava’s relationship with Laya is one of complement rather than contrast. Their shared aspect Karya is where art and engineering intertwine. Where Laya inspires, Tava shapes. Among the workshops of Kota Emas it is not unusual to see shrines to Karya. Yet while Laya is ancient and bound to the arts, Tava feels distinctly modern, a god who rose in prominence alongside the growing ambitions of Bandari invention. He is not a Deva of abstraction but of effort, sweat, tools, and the satisfaction of finished work.

Ritual-Charu-a76

Charu is a strategy board game invented in Bandar. Popular among the Bandari, one can find old men playing Charu in the parks of Kota Emas, or among bored sailors on long voyages. Unlike Kaigara, in which pieces are placed and stay their to build territory, Charu is played with carved pieces that move around the board attacking other pieces, each piece is unique and has different abilities and possible moves it can make. Players capture their opponents pieces and the game ends when one player captures the opponents "general." The most powerful piece on the board is the Luminary, which can move in any direction and be called back to the board under special circumstances, but is not the piece that decides the game.

Charu is a faster and more dynamic game than Kaigara, and while cheap travel versions are common, one can find expensive and elaborate Charu sets in the noble shops of Kota Emas where pieces are made of marble, obsidian, jade or even gold. A start contrast to games of Kaigara where even the king of the Kame Kingdom is known to play with simple cowrie shells and chalk.

Species-Anura-1b2

The Anura are one of the many non-human intelligent species found throughout Nuitai. They are amphibious humanoids with large, bulging eyes atop their heads and a fondness for cuisine that primarily features insects. Known for their sharp intelligence and diverse regional forms, they come in two varieties.

Ranagata

Small and agile, these amphibious natives inhabit the eastern and northeastern regions of Nuitai, particularly Māra Iti and the Lanuroa Archipelago. They have bright colorful skin that is slippery to the touch, and large webbed feet that makes them graceful swimmers and natural acrobats. None rival the Ranagata passion for motion; diving contests, canopy races and daring leaps between treetop bridges are common pastimes.

Ranagata have long co-existed with the native Nuitagata of the east and many practice Anúic traditions, typically as followers of Lanu. Fresh water is very important to the Ranagata, not just for drinking but for keeping their skin moist, and Lanu provides the gentle rain that nourishes the forests.

Ranagata typically build their homes in the high treetops of dense forests, with bridges connecting each structure creating floating villages in the canopies. Their native language is Rana'lo, characterized by rapid high pitched tonal ribbits that is a challenge for even the most skilled linguists to master.

Kaerujin

Where the Ranagata thrive among the trees and rain of the east, the Kaerujin embody a more earthen strength. Most Kaerujin live in the Kame Kingdom, though many can also be found in Kota Emas, where peoples of every kind intermingle.

They are typically larger than their Ranagata cousins, with dry bumpy skin, and are less acrobatic and slow moving, preferring to stay on the low earth than the treetops. They are more stocky and round than slim or sleek, and more muted in color. They could easily be mistaken for the Kame they live with if not for their lack of shells.

Despite their slower gait, they can be powerful warriors and a Kaerujin trained in Wai’Kenpō can be surprisingly agile and formidable in combat. While many Kaerujin practice the Kame spiritual tradition of Wai'Ki, they revere their own Anuran pantheon rather than the chelonian gods of the Kame. Kaerujin are naturally talented at long sessions of still, silent meditation, and have been mistaken for boulders during their practice.

The Kaerujin language, Kaerugo, is a sister language of Kamego. It shares the same traits of open vowels, a steady even flow of syllables, and intricate politeness levels of grammar, but punctuated by deep, slow and throaty croaking sounds.

Bandari biologists have speculated that Anura of Ranagata and Kaerujin ancestries have evolved from or share a common ancestor with tree frogs and toads, respectively.

Chelonian is a collective term for turtlefolk who inhabit the warm coastal waters and reefs around the Kame Kingdom and the northwestern seas of Nuitai. Common chelonians share deep ancestral ties with the Kame but follow a simpler way of life, shaped by the tides and currents.

Most Chelonian communities are nomadic sea-dwellers, traveling in small flotillas or living among coral islets. They possess a quiet, contemplative wisdom expressed through song, movement, and subtle changes in posture paired with low gurgling sounds rather than spoken language or writing.

Though they do not build kingdoms or complex societies, and nuanced communication can be a challenge, the Chelonians’ harmony with the ocean and their instinctive craftsmanship inspire respect among the Kame, who see in them a reflection of their own origins and the endurance of nature.

Like the Kame, chelonians are believed to also be descended from the great Dai-Kame, the ancient goliath sea turtles who roam the northern oceans as guardians of the deep.

The Pekapeka are chiropterans. Small, winged humanoids native to the deep jungles and limestone caverns of the Makua Archipelago . Technology-Lelua-8f6

Lelua is the fiery acrobatic martial art performed by the fire dancers of the Fire Islands, in southeast Nuitai. Supposedly they learned it directly from Mauga herself, or rather through her Akua Iti aspect Ahi, the personification of fire. This flashy kinesthetic art doubles as both a ritual dance honoring the goddess of volcanoes and a powerful fighting style, built on momentum, heat, and pure offense.

In combat, Lelua has almost no static defense. Its protection comes from constant motion, evasive spins, feints, and the sheer unpredictability of the user. It is fast, aggressive, and overwhelming by design.

Lelua reflects the nature of fire. It is explosive, unpredictable, emotional, and always moving. Practitioners channel emotions such as passion, rage, excitement, and raw will into their motions. There is no emphasis on blocking or guarding. The style is meant to overwhelm and confuse opponents through speed, intensity, and constant forward pressure. In its ritual forms, Lelua serves as an expression of devotion to Mauga, celebrating destruction, creation, and transformation. In battle, those same movements translate directly into devastating strikes.

Visually, Lelua is defined by acrobatic, dance-like motion. Practitioners rely on spins, rapid twirls, flying kicks, somersaults, cartwheels, falls, rolls, and sudden changes in direction. These movements build momentum for powerful attacks and make the fighter difficult to predict or pin down. Even without magic, Lelua is famous for being flashy, fast, and dangerous. It is also a very difficult martial art to master.

Most practitioners of Lelua use Mana simply to enhance their strength, stamina, and agility through the fire aspect of Mana. Devotional fire dancers, however, have refined their bodies and spirits enough to literally produce fire through movement. Transforming Mana into magic, flame bursts forth at the end of kicks, punches, or landings, and skilled dancers can even ignite their own bodies for short periods. This power carries real risk. The same fire that empowers them can consume them if they lose control. Much as the Callers risk possession or corruption by channeling Mana as Aku, shadow, channeling Mana as Ahi can result in self-immolation for the untrained.

Lelua is traditionally an unarmed form of combat, but it adapts well to lightweight weapons. With Bandari influence in the last century or so, fire dancers have been known to use swords, staffs, throwing knives, and other tools, lighting them on fire during performances or combat. These weapons are handled with the same acrobatic flair as the unarmed forms, sometimes juggled, spun, or thrown with impressive showmanship. While the people of the Fire Islands rejected Bandari religion and culture, they saw the utility of steel and adapted it to their martial arts.

The Fire Islands are famous throughout Nuitai for their fire dancers, and travelers sometimes encounter Lelua performers far from home. They can be seen as street performers as far west as Kota Emas . Some are trained dancers, others are simply enthusiasts who learned bits of the style abroad. Either way, a Lelua performance is something unforgettable: intense, bright, and impossible to ignore.
