# Jangya

Jangya is one of the many ancient Devas in the Sarpic pantheon. He is generally thought of as the god of war, a stereotype reinforced by his fearsome visage and battle hardened iconography, but followers of [Sarpa](../organization/sarpa.md) and devotees of Jangya in particular better understand that his domain encompasses those qualities that make great heroes and leaders such as courage, honor, discipline, inspiration, patience and clarity of judgement.

Affectionately known as the "Blue General", Jangya is depicted as an anthropomorphic elephant with azure skin and a powerful statue and build. He wears golden armor with a red and gold sash around his waste, a golden crown decorated with sapphires, rubies and emeralds, upon his head, and jewelry adorning his large ears, tusks and each of his six arms. Jangya is one of the most recognizable and memorable gods of the Sarpic pantheon due to his appearance, and is usually the first Deva outsiders to Sarpa think of despite not being the most important or central to the pantheon, he remains popular in the imagination of the larger Bandari population.

Like other Devas each pair of arms signifies a different aspect. Prajang is his aspect representing the courageous hero or the brave warrior, diving head first into battle with a curved sword and round, golden buckler in his lower hands. Where [Kong](../person/kong.md) is invoked when pure rage or strength are needed, warriors invoke Prajang for noble courage when immediate action is needed. The middle pair of arms belongs to Samanjang, the inspiring leader aspect of Jangya, occupying the space between mind and action. One hand gestures outward, urging and commanding soldiers forward to the battlefield, while the other is pulled back, gripped into a fist representing resolve. The top pair of arms symbolize Rananjang, the strategist and tactician. One hand holds a scroll while the other a [Charu](../ritual/charu.md) piece. Depictions of Prajang, especially of Rananjang in particular, are often of the god of war hunched over a game of Charu thoughtfully considering his next move, comically dwarfing his opponent in size if he is playing against a mortal, but still taking the game quite seriously, or patiently awaiting his opponents turn.

Jangya is not prayed to just by warriors and generals, but anyone seeking courage or decisiveness. His name is invoked casually in friendly games of strategy or competition in general, and Bandari in leadership positions will sometimes have an image of him decorating their offices. This is true mostly among Shakans, who make these gestures more as a token of symbolism or affection then religious devotion. As Sarpa is a dying religion, eclipsed by the Teachings of Shaka that is followed by the vast majority of Bandari, and only the ancestors of the Bandari living in the [Matahari Archipelago](../location/matahari-archipelago.md) have seen full scale war, there are no temples to Jangya and his devotees are few and far between. The proud descendants of warrior families or Sarpic sailors and adventurers braving the sea monster infested waters circling the great maelstrom may have shrines to him, as do politicians representing the Sarpic minority.

While Jangya is depicted as having the head and feet of an elephant, no one alive on the Matahari Archipelago would ever have seen one. Some flora and fauna made it on that voyage centuries ago, but most of the natural wonders of Bandar have been lost to legend. There are many depictions in art and mentions in historical or legendary accounts of beings that look like Jangya, blue skinned, multi-armed elephant men, but scholars believe are not Jangya. For one, these artistic depictions show multiple of these beings in one scene, and they are missing their divine symbology, and the texts that describe them are more descriptive than devotional, almost like a record of accounts. For this reason some believe there is a race of people who lived in Bandar, and possibly still exist in that forgotten realm, that inspired Jangya, perhaps he is an imported deity. These beings are sometimes referred to as simply "Jangs" or "Jangas."

The Matahari Archipelago, especially [Kota Emas](../settlement/kota-emas.md), is home to a wide variety of races. Among these are proboscideans, or "gajas" in Bhandhasa. Gajas have long prehensile noses, big floppy ears, and tusks, but do not have blue skin, they typically come in shades of grey or brown, they only have two arms, and they do not have divine or mystic powers. They are simply mortals living and working in the world like anyone else. The gaja minority has a complicated relationship with their resemblance to the ancient Sarpic god of war. For some, especially gajas that are devout followers of Shaka, they want nothing to do with any mention of Jangya or Jangs and can even find the comparison offensive. For some, including gajas that practice Sarpa, they may take the comparison in good humor and lean into the similarity, or for those few gajas that worship Jangya, consider it an honor or even claim divine lineage.

The pronunciation of Jangya's names in [Bandhasa](../language/bandhasa.md) vary by region. In the northern islands of the Matahari Archipelago, as well as Kota Emas, people tend to go by the shorter Jangya or simply Jang, and his aspects are known as Prajang, Samanjang and Rananjang. In the southern islands and the Bayang Seas polysyllabic pronunciations with open vowels are more common; Jangaya, Prajangaya, Samanjangaya, and Rananijangaya. Naturally, southern Bhandasa is spoken faster.
